Buying a Condo vs a House in Japan

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The buying rules are the same for both: no visa or residency is required, the brokerage cap is identical, and the same two post-purchase deadlines apply.
Where condo and house genuinely part company is in what you own, what you keep paying, and which checks can sink the purchase — the road rule can kill a house deal and is meaningless for a condo unit.
This is the comparison I built from the verified figures, including the one cost line where I found no honest average to print.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy either a condo or a house in Japan on the same national rules — no visa, residency, or Japanese bank account required, and ownership grants no visa. On a used condo, the closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price (SUUMO's closing-cost guide); on a used home, buyers commonly budget about 5–10% of the price for the closing costs, including the brokerage fee (SUUMO's closing-cost guide). The real differences are structural: a condo adds monthly management and repair-reserve charges set by the building's association, while a house puts the land, the road access, and every repair on you.

I started out treating "condo or house?" as a lifestyle question. Working through the documents turned it into a paperwork question instead: the two purchases share a process and then diverge in almost every line that follows the price. What follows is where they diverge, in the order I check them.

What you actually own

With a house, you own the building and the land under it, and the registration tax is charged on both assessed values — land transferred by sale at a reduced 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, a building at 2.0%. With a condo, you own the unit plus a share of the land and the common areas, and the same rates apply to those assessed values. One warning belongs here: some houses sit on leasehold land rather than land you own, which changes what you are really buying — I wrote that comparison up separately in the leasehold vs freehold guide, and I check the tenure before anything else.

The land also changes the annual tax arithmetic. Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, and residential land gets a reduced taxable base — one-sixth for the first 200 square metres per house, one-third above that. On a house with its own plot, that reduction is a real line in the calculation; on a condo unit it is mostly already reflected in a small land share.

The condo's extra monthly lines — and the average I will not print

A condo carries two recurring charges a house does not: a monthly management fee and a monthly repair-reserve contribution, both set by the building's owners' association. I went looking for a trustworthy national average for these and did not find one I could stand behind — the survey figures I chased were not retrievable from an official source, so this page prints no average. What I do instead is ask for the building's actual figures, the reserve fund's current balance, and its long-term repair plan, all of which belong in the important-matters explanation. A cheap unit in a building with an empty reserve fund is not cheap.

Related, and easy to miss: a buyer can end up responsible for a previous owner's unpaid condo management and repair-reserve fees. I covered that in its own guide — it is one of the first things I now verify on any condo, because the listing price never mentions it.

The house's extra checks

A house puts two legal checks on the table that a condo unit never raises. The first is the road: under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres, and a plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition. Exceptions exist, so I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation. The second applies to both types but bites older houses more often: the seismic standard changed in 1981, so I check the building confirmation date on anything older and treat a pre-change date as the start of the questions, not the end of them.

The house also has no association standing between you and a leaking roof. Every repair is yours, arranged by you or by the management company you hire — which is a choice with a condo and close to a necessity, from overseas, with a house.

Insurance, by structure class

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class, and is the same at every insurer — which is where the condo/house split shows up in the table: the highest band (Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa, Shizuoka) is ¥41,100 per ¥10,000,000 of cover per year for a wooden building and ¥27,500 for a non-wooden one, before discounts. A reinforced-concrete condo and a wooden house in the same city are priced in different classes. Fire insurance premiums are set freely by each insurer; no official figure exists, so I print none.

What is identical, and renting either out

The shared parts are not small: the deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price — under the same standard cancellation terms; the important-matters explanation is a legal duty before signing either contract; the timeline from offer to handover runs roughly one to three months, cash faster; and a non-resident buyer owes the same two filings afterwards — the FEFTA report within 20 days (not reporting carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000) and, from October 5, 2026, the nationality declaration at registration.

If the plan is to rent the property out, the machinery is the same for both: an MLIT survey found the most common management fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, making 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure, and rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding except when the tenant is an individual renting it as their own home. I make no promise about what rent either type will achieve in a given city — that is a local figure I will not invent. My own lean, for what it is worth, is boring: from overseas, the condo's association absorbs problems the house leaves on your desk — but I buy the building's documents before I buy that argument, every time.
