Buying Property in Japan with a Spouse: Joint Ownership

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Two names can go on the registration — nothing in the national rules stops a foreign couple from owning a Japanese property in shares.
The trap is not the registration; it is the money. If the shares on paper do not match who actually paid, the mismatch can be taxed as a gift.
Everything else — the brokerage cap, the deposit, the deadlines — runs as it does for a single buyer, except that the filings multiply by the number of owners.

Quick answer

Yes: spouses can buy together and register an ownership share for each of them, on the same national rules as any foreign buyer — no visa or residency required, and ownership grants no visa. The discipline is to register shares that match the money each of you actually contributes. A share registered to a spouse who did not pay for it can be treated as a gift of Japanese property to that spouse, and gifts of Japan property are taxable to the person who receives them even when both of you live abroad. The annual gift tax exemption is ¥1,100,000, and the lower special-rate table you may see quoted is for gifts from a parent or grandparent to an adult child — it does not apply between spouses. Each non-resident owner also owes their own FEFTA report within 20 days, and each registered owner makes the nationality declaration.

When my spouse and I first talked about buying, I assumed "joint ownership" was a box you tick at the brokerage. It is closer to a second, quieter transaction hiding inside the first: the purchase from the seller, and — if the money and the paperwork disagree — a transfer between the two of us that the tax office can see. What follows is how I lined the two up before signing anything.

How joint ownership is registered

Each owner is registered with a share — half and half, or any other split the two of you choose. Nothing in the buying rules requires a visa, residency, or a Japanese bank account from either of you, and the process around the registration is the single-buyer process: a licensed agent delivers the important-matters explanation before signing (a legal duty, not a courtesy), the deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price — under the standard terms that a cancelling buyer loses it and a cancelling seller repays double, and the timeline from offer to handover runs roughly one to three months, cash faster. Registration and license tax is charged on the fixed-asset-tax assessed values as usual — land transferred by sale at a reduced 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, a building at 2.0%.

Two filings attach to the people rather than the property. From October 5, 2026, each individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents — so a couple files it twice. And since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan, or rights in it, must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days. A registered share is rights in the property, so each non-resident spouse reports; not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

The money has to match the shares

Here is the part I nearly got wrong. If I pay the entire price from my account and we register half the property in my spouse's name, the half I paid for but registered to her can be treated as a gift from me to her. Japan taxes gifts of Japanese property to the person who receives the gift, and it does so even when the giver and the receiver are both non-residents — living abroad does not take the property out of the Japanese gift tax net. A gift tax return and payment fall due between February 1 and March 15 of the following year, filed through a tax agent in Japan if the recipient is a non-resident.

The reliefs are narrower than the articles I first read suggested. There is an annual exemption of ¥1,100,000 per recipient. Beyond that, the lower special-rate table that comes up in searches is reserved for gifts from a lineal ascendant — a parent or grandparent — to a recipient who is 18 or older on January 1 of the gift year. A spouse is not a lineal ascendant, so that table is not ours to use, and I do not budget with it. The working rules I took from this are plain: register shares that match the contributions, keep the bank records that show who paid what (sellers and agents commonly ask for statements and a record of where the money came from in any case), and if one of us is deliberately funding the other's share, treat that part as the gift it is and take advice on it before the registration, not after.

If one of you dies

This is headline only, because it stops being a buying question. Which country's law governs the succession itself is a separate question — in principle it follows the deceased's national law — and that is specialist territory I will not improvise. What Japan requires regardless is the registration: since April 1, 2024, registering an inheritance is mandatory, within three years of learning of it, with a fine of up to ¥100,000 for unjustified failure. The surviving spouse's own registered share is already theirs; it is the deceased's share that has to move.

The tax headline is also worth knowing before you choose the shares, not after. Japanese inheritance tax reaches property located in Japan regardless of the residency or nationality of the person who died or the person inheriting. The basic deduction is ¥30,000,000 plus ¥6,000,000 for each statutory heir, the return and payment are due within ten months of learning of the death (filed through a tax agent for a non-resident heir), and the 20% surtax that applies to heirs who are neither a spouse nor a first-degree blood relative does not fall on a spouse. None of that is a reason by itself to pick one split over another — it is a reason to pick the split with your eyes open and a specialist in the room.

If the plan is to rent it out

The rental machinery does not care that there are two of you. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. Rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family, and each owner files on their share of the income, with a tax agent appointed before filing. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.
