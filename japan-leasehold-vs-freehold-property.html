Leasehold vs Freehold in Japan: What Foreign Buyers Need to Know

A low asking price can make a Japanese property look like a bargain, but I would first check whether the purchase includes the land or only a leasehold right to use it. Freehold generally means owning the land and building; leasehold means owning the building while renting the land beneath it. That difference can affect ongoing costs, what needs the landowner’s consent, and how easily you can sell. I would also check what kind of leasehold is being offered and how much time remains on it.

What you own

With freehold, called shoyuken, you own both the land and the building permanently. There is no ground rent to a landowner, and you generally have greater freedom to sell or rebuild, within the law.

With leasehold, called shakuchiken, you have a right to use land belonging to someone else for the purpose of owning a building on it. You pay ground rent to the landowner. The building may be yours, but that does not make the land yours too.

I would ask for the ownership arrangement to be made clear before comparing the property’s price with other listings. A lower price is only one part of the picture when the rights and obligations are different.

Ground rent and landowner consent

Ground rent is an ongoing cost of a leasehold property. Its level is not something I can assume from the listing price; I would ask the licensed agent to confirm the amount and the terms for the particular property.

Selling or rebuilding can also require the landowner’s consent, or permission from a court. Consent fees for a transfer or rebuilding are customary in many cases. I would ask which consent and fees may apply to the property, rather than assume they are included in the price.

The level of ground rent and customary consent fees varies and is unverified here. A licensed agent or relevant professional can help you examine the applicable documents and costs.

Ordinary and fixed-term leaseholds

I would then check whether the land right is an ordinary leasehold or a fixed-term leasehold. The distinction matters because it changes what happens when the term reaches its end.

An ordinary leasehold is generally renewable in principle, unless the landowner has justifiable cause to oppose renewal. A fixed-term leasehold ends without renewal when its agreed term expires.

Depending on the fixed-term arrangement, there may be an obligation to remove the building and return the land cleared. I would ask a professional to identify the applicable arrangement and explain what the documents say about returning the land.

Why the remaining term matters

For a fixed-term leasehold, the remaining term is part of the purchase decision, not just a detail to file away. As the end approaches, the property’s value can decline, lenders may be more cautious about financing, and the pool of future buyers may narrow.

That does not tell me whether a particular property is a good or bad purchase. It tells me to ask a lender and the licensed agent how the remaining term affects financing and resale before making assumptions about an exit.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • Does the purchase include freehold land, or a leasehold right?
  • What ground rent applies, and what do the lease documents say about it?
  • Would a sale or rebuild require consent, and what fees could apply?
  • Is the leasehold ordinary or fixed-term, and how much of the term remains?
  • Does the arrangement require the building to be removed when it ends?
  • How might the remaining term affect financing and a future sale?

FAQ

Can I own the building without owning the land?

Yes. Under a leasehold arrangement, you can own the building while holding a right to use land owned by someone else.

Can an ordinary leasehold be renewed?

Generally, yes. Renewal is possible in principle unless the landowner has justifiable cause to oppose it.

Does a fixed-term leasehold always allow me to keep the building after it ends?

No. Depending on the contract arrangement, the building may have to be removed and the land returned cleared.
