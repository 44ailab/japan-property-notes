Buying Property in Yokohama as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Yokohama has no separate foreign-buyer permission: the rules that decide whether you can buy are national, and they do not require a visa or residency.
The one place Yokohama genuinely differs is the earthquake insurance table — Kanagawa sits in the most expensive premium band in the country, and that one I could read straight off the official table.
If you buy as a non-resident, two deadlines follow the purchase: a FEFTA report within 20 days, and an acquisition-tax bill that arrives months later.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Yokohama on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. The local difference is insurance: earthquake cover in Kanagawa is priced in the table's highest band, ¥41,100 per ¥10,000,000 of cover per year for a wooden building and ¥27,500 for a non-wooden one, before discounts.

Yokohama was the city where I expected the paperwork to be Tokyo paperwork with a different view. Most of it is. Working through it, I found exactly one line where the city itself changes a number I have to budget — and it was not a tax or a fee, it was the insurance table. What follows is the national process first, then that one local line.

What I checked first

The first thing I wanted to know was whether buying in Yokohama required any status I do not have. It does not. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The buying steps are the national ones

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. I read that document as the moment where the property's legal status, the road situation, and the fees are all supposed to be on the table, and I would not sign a contract that skipped it.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening. As a buyer who lives abroad, I plan on cash in any case: standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan, and there is no official program for non-residents. I treat the timeline as a planning range, not a promise — the individual case decides.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone, using the cap above. It is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. Without the two assessed values, any total I calculated from the price would be built on the wrong base, so I ask for them early. The annual side is the same shape: fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, the owner is whoever holds the property on January 1, and city planning tax can be added in urbanisation-promotion areas at up to 0.3% of the assessed value.

The acquisition tax is also the bill people forget. It does not arrive at the closing table; the prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase, in practice some months later — commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture. I keep it on the budget list even when the settlement statement looks complete.

The local difference: the insurance band

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class, and is the same at every insurer — which is why the prefecture matters. Kanagawa is grouped with Tokyo, Chiba, and Shizuoka in the table's highest band: ¥41,100 per ¥10,000,000 of cover per year for a wooden building, ¥27,500 for a non-wooden one, before discounts. The lowest band, covering 28 prefectures, is ¥11,200 and ¥7,300 on the same basis — so a wooden house in Yokohama carries close to four times the earthquake premium of the same house in a lowest-band prefecture, and no amount of shopping between insurers changes that, because the price is set by the table. Fire insurance is the opposite case: each insurer sets its own premiums, no official figure exists, and I print none.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

I do not manage a property from overseas myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
