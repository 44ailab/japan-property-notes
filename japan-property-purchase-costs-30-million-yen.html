At ¥30 million, I find it useful to separate the money needed around the transaction from costs that may arrive later. A single estimate can conceal both the timing and the different bases used to calculate the individual items.

The brokerage cap is not the whole cost

For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax.

Applying that formula to ¥30 million gives ¥960,000 before consumption tax. I would use it to understand the ceiling, then ask the brokerage what it will actually charge. The fee is one item in the purchase budget; it does not replace the registration, tax, or professional-fee questions.

The National Tax Agency’s reduced stamp-duty table places contract amounts over ¥10 million through ¥50 million in the ¥10,000 band. The reduced rates apply to contracts created by March 31, 2027. A ¥30 million contract sits within that band.

The deposit needs its own line

When I looked at the transaction process, I kept the deposit separate from the closing-cost estimates. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign.

A licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation before signing. I would take questions about the actual contract, payment schedule, and explanation to the licensed brokerage handling the transaction.

Ask for assessed values before estimating taxes

Registration and license tax is based on the fixed-asset-tax assessed value, not the ¥30 million purchase price. For land transferred by sale, the rate is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029. For a building transferred by sale, the rate is 2.0%.

Real estate acquisition tax is also based on assessed value. For acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, the reduced rate is 3% for land and houses; non-residential buildings remain at 4%. The taxable base for building land is halved under the applicable rules, and other reductions may exist. None of those figures alone tells me the tax on an unidentified property.

The prefecture sends its acquisition-tax notice after the purchase. Practitioner guidance commonly puts the notice at around three to six months later, although timing varies. I would not remove the item from my budget merely because it was absent from the closing table.

A planning range is a starting point

On a used condo, the closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price (SUUMO's closing-cost guide). On a used home, buyers commonly budget about 5–10% of the price for the closing costs, including the brokerage fee (SUUMO's closing-cost guide).

Same ¥30 million price, two different bills: used house vs used condo

If I were comparing a used house and a used condo at ¥30 million, I would not expect the purchase costs to be identical. Some lines start with the price, but others use the property’s fixed-asset-tax assessed value. That distinction matters when I try to plan the cash I need.

The price-based lines give me a starting point. At ¥30 million, the brokerage fee cap is ¥960,000 before consumption tax, or ¥1,056,000 including 10% consumption tax. That is the cap, not necessarily the fee charged. Stamp duty is ¥10,000: ¥30 million falls in the reduced-rate band over ¥10 million through ¥50 million. The reduced rates apply to contracts created by March 31, 2027.

Registration and license tax and real estate acquisition tax need a different calculation. Both use assessed value rather than the purchase price. For registration by March 31, 2029, the rate for land transferred by sale is 1.5%; the building rate is 2.0%. Acquisition tax is 3% for acquisitions by March 31, 2027, with the taxable base for residential land halved. I cannot calculate either tax from the ¥30 million price alone.

The assessed property also looks different in each case: a house is assessed as a whole land parcel plus a building, while a condo is assessed as one unit plus a share of the land. Acquisition tax is billed later by the prefecture, commonly about three to six months after purchase, although timing varies. I would keep money aside for that notice.

The judicial scrivener survey average is useful, but I would read the example before putting it in my budget. The national average of ¥56,678 including tax covers an ownership transfer involving one land parcel and one building, with a combined assessed value of ¥10 million. That fits the house pattern; it is not a quote for this purchase, and I would ask for a separate quote for a condo.

Earthquake insurance is the clearest annual difference I checked. It is an add-on to fire insurance, and the chosen cover is 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50 million for the building. That percentage is a cover limit, not a guaranteed payout. Per ¥10 million of cover per year, before discounts, the Tokyo/Chiba/Kanagawa/Shizuoka band charges ¥41,100 for wooden construction and ¥27,500 for non-wooden construction. In the lowest band, the figures are ¥11,200 and ¥7,300 respectively.

For a broader planning reference, SUUMO puts closing costs at about 6–8% of the price for a used condo and 5–10% for a used home, including the brokerage fee. At ¥30 million, those ranges are ¥1,800,000–¥2,400,000 and ¥1,500,000–¥3,000,000 respectively. I would use them as planning references, not quotes.

Three questions ¥30 million buyers ask

How much cash do I need on top of the price? The three starting lines I can put figures on are the brokerage fee cap including consumption tax (¥1,056,000), stamp duty (¥10,000), and the judicial scrivener survey average (¥56,678). Together they come to ¥1,122,678. This is not a final bill: the brokerage figure is a cap, and the scrivener figure is a benchmark based on a different assessed value. Registration and acquisition taxes sit on top and cannot be totalled without the assessed values. SUUMO’s planning ranges at ¥30 million are ¥1,800,000–¥2,400,000 for a used condo and ¥1,500,000–¥3,000,000 for a used home, including the brokerage fee.

When is each cost paid? Stamp duty is paid on the contract, and registration and license tax is paid at the ownership-transfer registration. Acquisition tax comes later by prefectural notice, commonly about three to six months after purchase, although timing varies. I would keep money aside for it rather than treating the registration payment as the end of the tax bill.

Why can’t I just add a percentage to the price and call it the total? The costs do not all use the same base. The brokerage cap starts with the price, while registration and acquisition taxes use assessed values. The cap also does not tell me the fee that will actually be charged. A planning percentage can help me set a range, but I would still check the individual figures and leave room for the acquisition tax notice that arrives later.

Frequently asked questions

How much are the closing costs when buying a ¥30 million house in Japan?

The lines fixed by the price are the brokerage fee cap of ¥1,056,000 including consumption tax, stamp duty of ¥10,000 at the reduced rate (contracts created by March 31, 2027), and the judicial scrivener fee at the national survey average of ¥56,678 including tax — ¥1,122,678 in total. Registration and license tax and real estate acquisition tax are added on top, because both are calculated on the assessed value rather than the price.

What is the brokerage fee on a ¥30 million property in Japan?

The legal cap is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. At ¥30 million that is ¥960,000 before consumption tax, or ¥1,056,000 including it. The cap is a ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction.

What taxes do I pay every year on property in Japan?

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, to the owner as of January 1 each year. In urbanisation-promotion areas, city planning tax is also charged at a rate set by the municipality, up to a cap of 0.3% of the assessed value. The bills are normally paid in four instalments across the year.

Do I have to declare my nationality when buying property in Japan?

Since October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality as search information when the ownership registration is filed, with supporting proof such as a passport copy. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, it is not a restriction on who can buy, and the declared nationality does not appear on the public registry certificate.
