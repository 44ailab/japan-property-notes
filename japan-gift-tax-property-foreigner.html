Gift Tax in Japan: Receiving Property as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Quick answer

A gift of property located in Japan can be subject to Japanese gift tax even if both the person giving it and the person receiving it live outside Japan. The recipient is responsible for the tax. There is an annual exemption of ¥1,100,000, and a non-resident recipient who must file or pay needs a tax agent in Japan.

Start with the location of the property

When I check this question, I start with where the property is, rather than with the passport of either person.

Japanese property is domestic-situs property. That means a gift of it can fall within Japan’s gift tax rules even when the donor and recipient are both non-residents. Moving the gift between family members who live abroad does not, by itself, put the property outside those rules.

The recipient is the person who needs to consider the gift tax. If someone plans to give you a Japanese property, I would raise the tax question before treating the transfer as complete.

Check the annual exemption and the rate category

The annual gift tax exemption is ¥1,100,000. That figure is an exemption; it is not a statement that every property gift will result in the same tax bill.

There is a special rate schedule for qualifying gifts from a lineal ascendant, such as a parent, to a recipient who is at least 18 years old on January 1 of the gift year. The special schedule has rates from 10% to 55%.

The relationship and the recipient’s age matter when checking whether that schedule applies. I would not choose a rate simply because the transfer is described as a family gift.

Plan the return and tax agent

The gift tax return and payment period is February 1 through March 15 of the year following the gift. For example, if a gift takes place during one calendar year, the stated filing period falls in the next calendar year.

A non-resident recipient who must file or pay in Japan appoints a Japan-resident tax agent and notifies the competent tax office. I would identify that person early, so the recipient has a way to handle the Japanese filing and payment.

The tax agent requirement is a separate point from the question of how much tax is due. Keep both on the checklist.

Keep gifts and inheritances separate

A property received as a gift and property received through an inheritance raise different tax questions. The exemption and special gift rates above belong to the gift tax discussion.

If the property will instead pass after a death, see the related page: Inheritance Tax in Japan for Foreign Heirs.

I would also keep the documents and the dates for the transfer together when asking a tax professional to check the facts. The relationship between the donor and recipient, the recipient’s age, and the year of the gift are particularly relevant to the special-rate conditions described here.

FAQ

Does a foreign passport prevent Japanese gift tax?

No. A gift of property located in Japan can be taxable to the recipient regardless of the parties’ residence.

Can a non-resident receive the property without considering Japanese gift tax?

No. Even when both donor and recipient are non-residents, the Japanese property is domestic-situs property and the gift can be taxable.

Does every gift from a parent qualify for the special rates?

Do not assume so. The stated conditions include a gift from a lineal ascendant and a recipient who is at least 18 on January 1 of the gift year.
