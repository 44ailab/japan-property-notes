Buying Property in Sapporo as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Sapporo follows the same national rules as everywhere else in Japan: no visa or residency is required to buy, and Hokkaido has no foreign-buyer permission of its own.
What is different up north is practical, not legal — a property you own from abroad has to survive a Hokkaido winter without you in it.
The figures below are the national ones I verified; the winter questions are the ones I added for Sapporo.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Sapporo on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies — worth knowing in Hokkaido, where low-priced houses are common.

When I started looking at Sapporo, my first assumption was that distance would be the problem — that owning in Hokkaido from overseas would somehow be a different legal exercise from owning in Tokyo or Osaka. It is not. The legal exercise is identical. What changed, the longer I looked, was my checklist: snow changes what you ask about the building, not what the law asks of you.

The rules are national — I checked for a Hokkaido layer and found none

Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. Ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan. "Non-resident" in the rules that follow is decided by where you live, not by your passport — a Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I settled which side of that line I was on first, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies to you.

Before signing, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end.

What winter adds to the checklist

None of this is law, and none of it comes with a figure I can print — it is the set of questions I now ask about any Sapporo property before I take the national numbers seriously. How is snow handled on the roof, and who clears it when the property is empty? What heating system is installed, and what does the building need so pipes do not freeze during a long absence? For a condo, what do the building's rules and fees say about winter maintenance? I put these to the agent during the important-matters explanation, because that is the meeting where the property's condition is supposed to be on the table — and I would rather hear an awkward answer there than discover the question after handover.

The annual bill, and the two deadlines after you buy

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value. The owner as of January 1 each year is the one taxed, the assessed value is reviewed every three years, and bills are normally paid in four instalments across the year. Residential land gets a reduced taxable base — the guide notes it exists rather than promising it for a given property.

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese; a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it, but I would confirm who is filing rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000. Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality does not appear on the public registry certificate.

Insurance, in a prefecture where the structure class matters

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class, and is the same at every insurer. I am not printing a Hokkaido premium here: the official table I verified names its bands by prefecture and structure, and the honest way to get Sapporo's figure is to read it off a quote for the actual building rather than have me guess at the band. Fire insurance premiums, by contrast, are set freely by each insurer — no official figure exists, so I print none.

If the plan is to rent it out

An empty property in a snowy city is exactly the case for a management company. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given Sapporo property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, the withheld amounts are credited, and before filing a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
