What Are the Annual Costs of Owning Property in Japan?

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

When I first sketched the cost of owning a place in Japan, my spreadsheet had one recurring line: the property tax, 1.4%. That was the wrong picture, and it was wrong in a specific way — not because the 1.4% was incorrect, but because it was alone. Checking the actual list, the holding costs are several separate mechanisms with separate bases: a tax on assessed value, a second smaller tax in some areas, a management percentage if the property is rented, insurance priced by prefecture and structure, and — for a condominium — the building's own monthly charges. This page puts the recurring list on one page. It deliberately does not add the items into a single annual total, because the bases differ and a summed figure would be invented, not calculated.

Quick answer

The recurring costs of owning property in Japan are: fixed asset tax at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value; city planning tax of up to 0.3% in urbanisation-promotion areas; if the property is rented out, management at a practical planning figure of 3–5% of the rent; fire and earthquake insurance premiums set by insurer, prefecture, and structure; and, for a condominium, monthly management and repair-reserve charges set by the building. One-time purchase costs — acquisition tax, registration tax, brokerage — are not annual costs, however often they are confused with them.

The tax that anchors the list

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value. Two details decide what you actually pay. The base is the assessed value, not the purchase price — the two are different numbers, and the assessed value is reviewed every three years. And the taxpayer is whoever owns the property on January 1 each year, with the bill normally paid in four instalments across the year (the months vary by municipality). Residential land gets a reduced taxable base — one-sixth for the first 200 square metres per house, one-third above that — which is one reason two properties at the same price can carry different tax bills. City planning tax rides on the same assessed value where it applies: only in urbanisation-promotion areas, at a rate the municipality sets up to a cap of 0.3%.

If the property is rented: the management percentage

An owner living overseas does not collect rent and chase plumbers personally, so management is a real recurring cost. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that fees are charged as a percentage of rent by about 80% of companies, and the most common band is 3% to under 5% of the rent — which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. It is a survey band, not a quote: the contract for the actual property sets the number. Separate from cost, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer in most cases — that is a tax mechanism on income, settled in the owner's Japanese return, and I list it here only so it is not mistaken for a fee.

Insurance: two different mechanisms

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building and ¥10,000,000 for household goods. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. Its premium depends only on prefecture and structure class and is the same at every insurer; the official table names the highest band (Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa, Shizuoka: ¥41,100 wooden / ¥27,500 non-wooden per ¥10,000,000 of cover per year, before discounts) and the lowest band (¥11,200 / ¥7,300). Fire insurance itself is priced freely by each insurer by building, location, and cover — no official figure exists, and any page that prints one as a standard is inventing it. Budget insurance as a quote to obtain, not a constant.

The condominium line I cannot put a number on

A condominium unit adds monthly management fees and repair-reserve contributions, set by the building's management association. I am often asked what is normal. The honest answer is that the survey averages for these charges are not in my verified fact base, so this page prints none. The building's own documents — the fee schedule, the reserve balance, the long-term repair plan — are the source, and reading them before purchase is part of the buying checks on this hub, not an optional extra.

What is NOT an annual cost

Three purchase-time costs are regularly confused with holding costs. Real estate acquisition tax is one-time — 3% for land and houses on acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, on the assessed value, billed by prefectural notice some months after the purchase. Registration and license tax is one-time, on the assessed value (land 1.5% on sale registrations made by March 31, 2029; building 2.0%). The brokerage fee is one-time, capped at 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax for a property priced above ¥4,000,000. They belong in the purchase budget, not in this list — mixing the two lists is how both budgets end up wrong.

Sources: Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications fixed asset tax and city planning tax overviews; National Tax Agency: registration and license tax; MLIT FY2025 survey of rental management companies; Ministry of Finance earthquake insurance overview and premium table — as set out in this hub's fact base.
