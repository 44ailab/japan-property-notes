Buying Property in Hiroshima as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Hiroshima has no separate foreign-buyer permission: the rules that decide whether you can buy are national, and they do not require a visa or residency.
What pulled me toward Hiroshima was the older housing stock — which moves the real work from the price lines to two checks: the road the plot touches, and the seismic standard the building was confirmed under.
If you buy as a non-resident, two deadlines follow the purchase: a FEFTA report within 20 days, and an acquisition-tax bill that arrives months later.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Hiroshima on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. On older Hiroshima stock, the checks matter more than the fee lines: a plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres or it generally cannot be rebuilt, and the seismic standard changed in 1981, so the building confirmation date is one of the first things to read.

I came to Hiroshima for the reason a lot of overseas buyers do: the listings were older, cheaper-looking houses rather than new condos. Pricing one properly turned out to be less about the price and more about two documents — the road record and the building confirmation. What follows is what I checked, in the order I now check it.

What I checked first

The first thing I wanted to know was whether buying in Hiroshima required any status I do not have. It does not. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The buying steps are the national ones

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. On older stock I read that document as the moment where the two checks below are supposed to be answered: the property's legal status, the road situation, and the fees are all supposed to be on the table, and I would not sign a contract that skipped it.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening. I treat that as a planning range, not a promise — the individual case decides.

The two checks older stock forces

The first is the road. Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition — the house can stand, but once it comes down, a new one may not go up. Exceptions exist, so the word is "generally", and I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation rather than measure the lane myself. Older Hiroshima neighbourhoods are exactly where narrow lanes and old plots meet, so this check comes before any enthusiasm about the price.

The second is the seismic line. Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, so on any older building I check the building confirmation date and treat a date from before the change as the start of the questions, not the end of them. A cheap old house that cannot be rebuilt and was confirmed under the old standard is not the same purchase as the listing photo suggests.

Context for why so much of the stock looks like this: the official 2023 Housing and Land Survey counted 9,002,000 vacant homes in Japan — 13.8% of all homes, 3,856,000 of them with no rental, sale, or second-home use. What the survey does not do is price them: no official price series for vacant homes exists, so I describe Hiroshima prices qualitatively and treat any single asking price as one seller's example, never as a market statistic.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone, using the cap above — with one addition that matters at Hiroshima's lower price points: for properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies. The cap is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. The judicial scrivener who handles that registration charges around ¥57,000 on average in the national federation's survey, plus small document costs; there is no official fee schedule, and the quote varies by office and case. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027 — and it does not arrive at the closing table. The prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase, in practice some months later, commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture. I keep it on the budget list even when the settlement statement looks complete.

Insurance follows the same discipline. Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class and is the same at every insurer; the verified table I worked from names the highest band and the lowest band covering 28 prefectures, and I will not guess Hiroshima's place between them — the exact band comes out of the official table when the policy is quoted. Fire insurance premiums are set freely by each insurer; no official figure exists, so I print none.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

I do not manage a property from overseas myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
