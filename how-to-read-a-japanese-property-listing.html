How to Read a Japanese Property Listing

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The first Japanese listing I ever opened, I read the way I would read one at home: price, photos, floor area, done. The price was the only number I actually understood. Everything that would later matter — the age line, the area figure that quietly leaves the balcony out, the two monthly charges hiding under a condo's price — was sitting on the page in code, and I skimmed straight past it. This page is how to read a Japanese property listing the second way: the way I read them now.

Quick answer

A Japanese listing is a compressed document. The floor-plan code (such as 2LDK) describes the layout, the stated floor area is the exclusive area and usually excludes the balcony, the building's age line is really a question about which side of the 1981 seismic-standard change its building confirmation falls on, and the walking minutes to the station follow standardised display rules, so they can be compared between listings. The price is only the starting figure — fees and taxes are calculated separately, and the legal record of what you are buying is not the listing but the important-matters explanation a licensed agent must deliver before you sign.

When I started, my mistake was treating the listing as the document. It is an advertisement written to a standard format — which is good news, because a standard format can be learned once and then read quickly forever. What follows is the order I now read the fields in, and the places where I slow down.

The floor-plan code is a sentence, not a label

The code counts rooms and then describes the shared space. A 1K is one room with a kitchen corner; a 1LDK is one separate room plus a combined living-dining-kitchen; a 3LDK is three rooms plus that shared space. An S in the code usually marks a small extra room — storage or a service room — that does not count as a full room. Room sizes are given in square metres and often also in tatami-mat units (jō). The tatami unit is a traditional measure whose exact size varies, so when I compare two homes I compare the square-metre figures and treat the tatami count as a feel for the room, not a measurement.

Why does the home feel bigger than the area figure?

Because the photograph includes the balcony and the figure usually does not. The stated area is the exclusive area — the space inside the unit that is yours. Balconies are normally shown as their own line, and on a condo they are shared-property space you use exclusively, not part of the exclusive area you own. I got this wrong in my head for weeks, comparing a Japanese condo's exclusive area against floor areas at home that are measured differently, and wondering why everything looked small on paper and fine in person. The age line is really a question about 1981

Every listing states when the building was built. What I actually need from that line is not the age itself but which seismic standard the building was confirmed under. Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, and the building confirmation date — not the completion date — is what places a building on one side of that line or the other. A home built just before the change and one built just after can look identical in photographs and be very different purchases. I never take the year on the listing as the answer; I ask for the confirmation date and check it against the standard. My page on the 1981 seismic standard works through that check in full.

Can I trust the walking minutes?

More than I expected to. The access line gives the nearest station and a walking time, and those minutes are produced under standardised display rules rather than each seller's optimism, which makes them comparable across listings in a way that "quiet neighbourhood" never is. What the minutes do not tell me is the walk itself — hills, level crossings, unlit streets — so for a property I am serious about, the minutes get me to a shortlist and a map check does the rest.

A condo listing carries two extra numbers

Under a condo's price sit two monthly charges: the management fee and the repair-reserve contribution. They are real, recurring costs of owning that unit, and they vary building by building. I will not print an "average" figure for them here, because the survey averages I went looking for could not be verified — and a wrong average is worse than none. What I do instead is ask, for any condo I take seriously, for the building's actual figures, its reserve balance, and its long-term repair plan, all of which belong in the important-matters explanation. One more line to check on a used condo: whether the previous owner is behind on those charges, because a buyer can end up liable for a previous owner's unpaid condo fees. That risk has its own page in this hub, and it is the listing question I would least want to skip.

For a house, the land lines matter more than the house lines

A house listing also describes the plot, and one field there can decide the property's whole future: the road it touches. Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails that test generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition — the existing house can stand, but the land's next chapter is heavily restricted. Listings do not always shout this, so I look for the road width and frontage figures, and I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation rather than guessing from a map.

The listing is the advertisement; the explanation is the record

Everything above is about reading the advertisement well. The document that legally describes what you are buying comes later: before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. That is where the legal status, the road situation, the charges and the fees are supposed to be on the table in full. I read listings to build a shortlist and to know which questions to ask; I read the explanation to decide.

One last habit: when a listing aimed at investors shows a return figure, I treat it as the seller's presentation, not a promise. The costs that come out of any rent — management, withholding tax, the annual property taxes — are set out in my other pages, and they are the difference between a headline figure and an income.
