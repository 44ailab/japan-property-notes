Buying Property in Shizuoka as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

People come to Shizuoka for the view of Mount Fuji, and I was no different. The number that stopped me was not a property price — it was an insurance premium. On the official earthquake insurance table, Shizuoka does not sit with the quiet prefectures. It sits in the highest band in the country, in the same group as Tokyo, Chiba and Kanagawa, and that one line in a government table changed how I ran every number afterwards. This page is buying property in Shizuoka as a foreigner with that table up front, and the national rules and costs that follow.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Shizuoka on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Earthquake insurance here is priced in the official table's highest band: ¥41,100 a year per ¥10,000,000 of cover for a wooden building, ¥27,500 for a non-wooden one.

When I first looked at Shizuoka, I assumed a prefecture of tea fields and coastline would price its risks gently — that stepping away from the capital would soften the running costs along with the prices. I checked the earthquake insurance table, and found the opposite: on this one cost, Shizuoka is grouped with Tokyo itself. The purchase rules are national, as everywhere; what is local is that band, and it belongs in the budget from the first day.

Do I need a visa or residency to buy here?

No. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The earthquake table, in full

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class, and is the same at every insurer, which is why a single official table settles the question.

On that table, Shizuoka sits in the highest band in the country, alongside Tokyo, Chiba and Kanagawa: ¥41,100 per year for every ¥10,000,000 of cover on a wooden building, and ¥27,500 on a non-wooden one. For contrast, the lowest band — 28 prefectures — pays ¥11,200 wooden and ¥7,300 non-wooden for the same cover. A wooden house in Shizuoka therefore carries close to four times the earthquake premium of the same house in a lowest-band prefecture, every year, for as long as you insure it. That is not a reason to walk away from the prefecture; it is a number to put in the spreadsheet before the scenery puts itself in yours. Fire insurance premiums, by contrast, are set freely by each insurer, and no official figure exists for those.

What happens between offer and keys?

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. That document is where the property's legal status, the road situation, and the fees are all supposed to be on the table, and I would not sign a contract that skipped it.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening. A buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash — standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan. I treat the timeline as a planning range, not a promise.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone, using the cap above. It is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. The judicial scrivener who handles the registration is a separate cost: the national scrivener federation's fee survey puts the average for an ownership-transfer registration at around ¥57,000, tax included, for a standard case — your quote varies by office and case.

Two costs keep running after the purchase. Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, billed to whoever owns the property on January 1 and normally paid in four instalments across the year; city planning tax can add up to 0.3% in urbanisation-promotion areas. And the acquisition tax is the bill people forget: it does not arrive at the closing table — the prefecture sends a payment notice some months later, commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

Shizuoka is not a prefecture I can manage a property in from overseas by myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
