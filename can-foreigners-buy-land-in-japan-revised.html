Can Foreigners Buy Land in Japan?

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The question I hear most from buyers overseas is not about price. It is whether the ground itself is off limits — can foreigners buy land in Japan at all, or is ownership quietly reserved for citizens? I assumed there would be a restriction buried somewhere, the way many countries reserve farmland or border areas. When I checked, the answer was simpler than the rumour, and the real checks turned out to be about the plot, not the passport. This page is what I found.

Quick answer

Yes. A foreigner can buy land in Japan on the same rules as a Japanese buyer — no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own, and owning land gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. The checks that actually decide a land purchase are about the plot: whether it touches a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres (or it generally cannot be rebuilt on), whether it sits in a designated watch zone near a sensitive facility (a prior notification for plots of 200㎡ or more), and — for non-resident buyers — the report to the Minister of Finance within 20 days of the acquisition.

One scope note before the detail: this page covers ordinary building land and houses. Farmland is a separate legal category that sits outside the fact base I have verified, so I leave it out rather than guess.

Ownership is about residence, not nationality

Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own land or buildings. Where the rules do draw a line, it is not at nationality: "non-resident" is decided by where you live. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. The reporting duty below follows that line, not your passport.

The other side of the coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a land purchase as a route to living here.

Check one: can anything be built on it again?

Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails that test generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition: an existing house can stand and be repaired, but its replacement cannot. Cheap rural land is sometimes cheap for exactly this reason, and the listing price will not tell you. Exceptions and approvals exist, so I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation — the document a licensed agent must deliver before you sign — rather than measuring a street on a map and guessing.

Check two: is the plot inside a watch zone?

Under the Act on the Survey and Regulation of Land Use Around Important Facilities, land and buildings inside a designated Special Watch Zone are subject to one extra step: for a transfer of 200㎡ or more, both seller and buyer must notify the Prime Minister in advance. I want to be precise about what this is, because it is often misreported: it is a notification, not an approval, and it applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike. It does not block a purchase; it adds a filing before one. If a plot I was looking at sat inside a designated zone, the practical effect would be timing, not eligibility.

Check three: the 20-day report for non-residents

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan — land included — must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

What land costs beyond the price

For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax; for properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies. Registration and license tax on land transferred by sale uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price, at the reduced rate of 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value — for building land, the taxable base is halved for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027 — and its payment notice arrives from the prefecture some months after the purchase, commonly about three to six months, rather than at the closing table.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign.

Sources: Ministry of Finance: FEFTA reporting for non-residents acquiring real property, Ministry of Justice: nationality declaration at registration, National Tax Agency: registration and license tax, LIFULL HOME'S: the road-access rule.
