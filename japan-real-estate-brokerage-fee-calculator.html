Japan Real Estate Brokerage Fee Calculator for Buyers

For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax.
For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies.
The cap is a maximum, not a promise that every buyer will pay that amount.

When I started looking at Japanese property costs, I wanted a calculator that would turn the asking price into a clear figure. The brokerage fee looked like a good place to start. Then I noticed that the price condition matters, and that the quoted formula does not cover every purchase in the same way.

The first question I would ask is whether a brokerage fee is payable in the transaction at all. If it is, ask the licensed brokerage for its fee quotation and how consumption tax is included. A legal ceiling gives you a boundary; it does not replace a written quote for your purchase.

Using the formula

For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax.

Take a ¥10,000,000 price. The percentage component is ¥300,000. Add ¥60,000 and the result is ¥360,000 before consumption tax.

At ¥30,000,000, the percentage component is ¥900,000. Add ¥60,000 and the result is ¥960,000 before consumption tax.

Those calculations show the cap before tax, not a complete purchase budget. They also assume the stated purchase price is the relevant figure for the calculation. Ask the brokerage to confirm its calculation and the amount you would actually owe.

The lower-price ceiling needs attention

For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies.

That separate ceiling is one reason I would not paste the general formula into a spreadsheet and assume it resolves every low-priced purchase. Ask the brokerage which ceiling applies to your transaction and request the amount in writing. Do not treat ¥330,000 as an automatic charge.

What the calculator leaves out

A brokerage fee is only one possible cost. Registration and license tax is based on the government’s fixed-asset-tax assessed value, not simply the purchase price. A judicial scrivener may charge separately for registration work. Stamp duty can apply to a sale contract, and a prefectural real estate acquisition tax notice can arrive after purchase.

For a broader planning reference, SUUMO’s guide gives an estimate for a used condo: closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price. That is a planning estimate for a used condo, not an additional fee to add on top of every item in a detailed quote.

I would keep the brokerage quote and the other estimates on separate lines. That makes it easier to see whether a total already includes the fee and avoids counting it twice.

Questions I would take to the brokerage

  • Is a brokerage fee payable by me in this transaction?
  • What is the fee before consumption tax, and what is the total including tax?
  • If the property is priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, how does the separate ceiling apply?
  • Which other charges are included in your estimate, and which will be billed separately?

SUUMO's closing-cost guide is a useful starting point. For an actual transaction, the brokerage’s itemised quote is more useful than a calculator alone.
