Buying Property in Nagoya as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Nagoya has no separate foreign-buyer permission: the rules that decide whether you can buy are national, and they do not require a visa or residency.
Nagoya is where I stopped asking "what does it cost?" in general and started pricing a purchase line by line — the brokerage cap, the stamp duty, and the scrivener's average can all be worked out before you ever visit.
The two taxes that cannot be priced from the listing — registration and acquisition tax — need the assessed values, so I ask for those early.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Nagoya on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. On a sale contract between ¥10,000,000 and ¥50,000,000, the stamp duty is ¥10,000 at the reduced rate for contracts created by March 31, 2027.

My first Nagoya spreadsheet was wrong in a way I only caught later: I had priced the whole purchase as percentages of the listing price. Some costs really do work that way. Others are fixed by a table, and two of the biggest are calculated on a number that appears nowhere in the listing. This page is the corrected version — what can be priced in advance, and what cannot.

What I checked first

The first thing I wanted to know was whether buying in Nagoya required any status I do not have. It does not. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The costs I could price exactly

The brokerage fee is a legal ceiling I can calculate from the price: for a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. A cap is not a quote, so I still ask the brokerage for its actual fee — but the cap tells me the most the line can come to.

The stamp duty on the sale contract comes from a table, not a percentage. At the reduced rates for contracts created by March 31, 2027: over ¥5,000,000 to ¥10,000,000 is ¥5,000; over ¥10,000,000 to ¥50,000,000 is ¥10,000; over ¥50,000,000 to ¥100,000,000 is ¥30,000. Small money next to the price, but it is one of the few lines I could fix to the yen before signing anything.

The judicial scrivener who handles the registration has no official fee schedule — each office sets its own. The best benchmark I found is the Japan Federation of Shiho-shoshi Associations' fee survey: an average of ¥56,678, tax included, for an ownership-transfer registration on a standard sale. I budget around ¥57,000 on average in the national survey, plus small document costs, and treat the actual quote as the real figure.

The two costs the listing price cannot tell you

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. Without the assessed values, any total built from the price alone is built on the wrong base.

The acquisition tax is also the bill people forget. It does not arrive at the closing table; the prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase — in practice some months later, commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture. It stays on my budget list even when the settlement statement looks complete.

The process, and paying for it

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening.

On financing, the honest position is the limited one: standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan, and the foreigner-friendly lenders serve foreign residents. A buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash. On paying, there is no official fee table for international wires and prices change, so I describe the method and the questions to ask rather than print a provider's fee. Sellers and agents commonly ask for bank statements and a record of where the money came from — I had mine ready before I was asked.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.
