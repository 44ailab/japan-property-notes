Buying Property in Nagano as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

"Nagano" is the slipperiest name on this hub. The prefecture is large and mountainous, the capital is Nagano City, and listings use the one word for both — a property an hour from the city can still arrive in a search labelled Nagano. Before I could ask my usual question — what does buying property in Nagano as a foreigner actually require? — I had to pin down which Nagano a listing meant. The purchase itself runs on the same national rules as anywhere in Japan. The check that matters here is matching the search word to the municipality and parcel in the official documents.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Nagano on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price.

Do I need a visa or residency to buy here?

No. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The check that matters: which Nagano is it?

The registry, the fixed-asset-tax assessment, and the important-matters explanation all name the actual municipality and parcel — the listing headline does not decide anything. I verify that the documents put the property in the municipality I searched for, and I resolve any gap between the headline and the documents before the deposit, not after. Two document checks ride along with that one in a prefecture of older mountain housing: the building confirmation date, which places a house against the 1981 change in Japan's seismic standard, and the road status — under the Building Standards Act a plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres, or it generally cannot be rebuilt. Both belong in the important-matters explanation, and I ask for them there rather than inferring either from photographs.

What happens between offer and keys?

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. That document is where the municipality, the confirmation date, the road status, and the fees are all supposed to be on the table, and I would not sign a contract that skipped it.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening. A buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash — standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan. I treat the timeline as a planning range, not a promise.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone, using the cap above. The cap is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. The judicial scrivener who handles the registration is a separate cost: the national scrivener federation's fee survey puts the average for an ownership-transfer registration at around ¥57,000, tax included, for a standard case — your quote varies by office and case.

One cost keeps running after the purchase. Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, billed to whoever owns the property on January 1 and normally paid in four instalments across the year; city planning tax can add up to 0.3% in urbanisation-promotion areas. And the acquisition tax is the bill people forget: it does not arrive at the closing table — the prefecture sends a payment notice some months later, commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture.

What about earthquake insurance in Nagano?

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class and is the same at every insurer — but the official premium table in my fact base names only the highest band and the lowest band, and Nagano is in neither of the named lists, so I will not print a Nagano premium figure I cannot source. Fire insurance premiums are set freely by each insurer, and no official figure exists for those either. What I can do — and did — is understand the mechanism before asking an insurer for the actual quote.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

Managing a property in Nagano from overseas is not something I can do by myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
