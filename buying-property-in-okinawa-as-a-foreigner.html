Buying Property in Okinawa as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Okinawa has no separate foreign-buyer permission: the rules that decide whether you can buy are national, and they do not require a visa or residency.
Two things are genuinely different at this distance: you will run everything through other people, and land near designated important facilities carries a notification rule that is worth checking before you fall for a plot.
If you buy as a non-resident, two deadlines follow the purchase: a FEFTA report within 20 days, and an acquisition-tax bill that arrives months later.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Okinawa on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. One Okinawa-specific check: inside a designated Special Watch Zone, a transfer of land or buildings of 200 square metres or more requires prior notification by both seller and buyer — a notification, not a permission, and it applies regardless of nationality.

Okinawa was the first place where I stopped reading the national checklist and started asking what changes when the property is an island away from everyone involved in selling it to me. Two things changed: one rule I had not met on the mainland, and the simple fact that every practical step would be done by someone else, on my instructions, at a distance. What follows is both.

What I checked first

The first thing I wanted to know was whether buying in Okinawa required any status I do not have. It does not. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

The check most mainland guides skip

Okinawa hosts a concentration of important facilities, and Japanese law watches the land around them. Under the Act on the Survey and Regulation of Land Use Around Important Facilities, areas around designated facilities are set as watch zones. Inside the stricter category, a Special Watch Zone, a transfer of land or buildings of 200 square metres or more requires prior notification — by both the seller and the buyer — to the Prime Minister. Three things about that rule are easy to get wrong, so I checked them carefully: it is a notification, not a permission, and nobody is granting or refusing your purchase; it applies regardless of nationality, to Japanese buyers exactly as much as to me; and it bites only at or above the 200-square-metre threshold inside a designated zone, not across Okinawa generally. The zones are designated and published by the Cabinet Office, and whether a specific plot sits inside one is a question I put to the agent for the important-matters explanation, before signing anything.

Buying and owning at this distance

The steps themselves are the national ones. Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening — and as a non-resident I plan on cash, since standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan.

Distance changes the weight of the management decision. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. With a house on the mainland I could at least imagine flying in to deal with a problem myself; with Okinawa, the management company is not a convenience, it is the operating model, and I budget the fee from day one. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone, using the cap above. It is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. Without the two assessed values, any total I calculated from the price would be built on the wrong base, so I ask for them early. The acquisition tax also does not arrive at the closing table; the prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase, in practice some months later — commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture. I keep it on the budget list even when the settlement statement looks complete.

Insurance is where island weather makes buyers ask for numbers I cannot honestly give. Fire insurance premiums are set freely by each insurer for the building, its location, and its cover; no official figure exists, so I print none and take the quote as the quote. Earthquake insurance is the structured half: it is an add-on to fire insurance, you set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building — that percentage is the cover limit you choose, not a guaranteed payout — and the premium depends only on prefecture and structure class, the same at every insurer. The verified table I worked from names the highest band and the lowest; I do not assign Okinawa a band figure I have not verified.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

On the tax side of renting, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.

Sources: Ministry of Finance: FEFTA reporting for non-residents acquiring real property, Ministry of Justice: nationality declaration at registration, National Tax Agency: registration and license tax, Important Land Act watch-zone rules, compiled from Cabinet Office publications.
