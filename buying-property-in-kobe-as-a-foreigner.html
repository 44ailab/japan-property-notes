Buying Property in Kobe as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Kobe follows the same national rules as everywhere else in Japan: no visa or residency is required to buy, and Hyogo has no foreign-buyer permission of its own.
What Kobe made me careful about was age. In a port city with deep older neighbourhoods, the checks that mattered were the building's confirmation date and whether the land can legally be rebuilt on.
The annual taxes are the national ones; I set out the rates I verified and who actually receives the bill.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Kobe on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, and a plot that does not touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres generally cannot be rebuilt.

My first Kobe shortlist was built the way I built every shortlist: price, size, distance to a station. Two of the older houses on it taught me to sort differently. One had a building confirmation from before the seismic standard changed in 1981. The other sat on a lane so narrow that rebuilding after demolition was in doubt. Neither fact was in the listing headline; both were exactly what the important-matters explanation exists to surface. This page is the checklist that came out of that.

What I checked first

The first thing I wanted to know was whether buying in Kobe required any status I do not have. It does not. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. Ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan. "Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport — a Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I settled which side of that line I was on first, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

Before signing, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end.

Older buildings: the two checks I now run first

The seismic standard changed in 1981, so for any older building I check the building confirmation date and treat a pre-change confirmation as a prompt for harder questions — about the structure, about any seismic work done since, and about how the agent is presenting it in the important-matters explanation. I am not an engineer and this page is not a survey; it is the point at which I stop relying on the listing and start relying on documents.

The second check is the road. Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition. Exceptions exist, so I say "generally" — and I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation rather than measure the lane myself. In older districts this single fact can matter more than anything in the photographs.

The annual taxes, and the bill that arrives late

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value. The owner as of January 1 each year is the one taxed, the assessed value is reviewed every three years, and bills are normally paid in four instalments across the year. City planning tax applies only in urbanisation-promotion areas, at a rate set by the municipality up to a cap of 0.3% of the assessed value. Residential land gets a reduced taxable base — one-sixth for the first 200 square metres per house — which is worth knowing on a house plot and irrelevant on most condo units.

Real estate acquisition tax is a one-time prefectural tax on the assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. Its bill does not arrive at the closing table: the prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase, in practice some months later — commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture. Registration and license tax also uses the assessed value, not the price: land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

I do not manage a property from overseas myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given Kobe property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, the withheld amounts are credited, and before filing a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
