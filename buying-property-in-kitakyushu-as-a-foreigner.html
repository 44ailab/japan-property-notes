Buying Property in Kitakyushu as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The listings that stop people in Kitakyushu are the prices — houses advertised for less than a family car — and the first reaction is usually suspicion: what is wrong with it? Sometimes the answer is something real: an old confirmation date, a plot that cannot be rebuilt. But sometimes the answer is just arithmetic. This is a city built on steel that has been losing people for decades, in a country that counted 9,002,000 vacant homes — 13.8% of every home in Japan. This page is buying property in Kitakyushu as a foreigner, on telling supply-driven cheap from document-driven cheap.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Kitakyushu on the same national rules as anywhere else in Japan. No visa, residency, or Japanese bank account is required to own, and owning gives you no visa or right to live in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies — the version of the rule that governs much of this city's stock. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price.

When I first looked at Kitakyushu, I read every low price as a warning sign. Checking the national figures changed that: the 2023 Housing and Land Survey counted 9,002,000 vacant homes across Japan, 13.8% of the entire housing stock, and 3,856,000 of them were not being rented, sold, or used as second homes at all. A former industrial city sits at the sharp end of that arithmetic. There is no official price series for vacant homes, so I describe prices here qualitatively — but the supply behind them is a published number, and it explains a great deal of what the listings show.

Do I need a visa or residency to buy here?

No. Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. The other side of that coin matters just as much: ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan, so I never treat a purchase as a route to living here.

"Non-resident" for the reporting rules is decided by where you live, not by your passport. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. I worked out which side of that line I was on before anything else, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

Telling the two kinds of cheap apart

Supply-driven cheap needs no apology: too many houses, not enough households, and a price that reflects it. Document-driven cheap is different — the price is low because of something in the paperwork that limits what the buyer can ever do with the property. Two checks separate them.

The first is the confirmation date. Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, and the building confirmation date — not the year in the listing headline — places a house on one side of that change or the other. A city of Kitakyushu's age has a deep stock of older houses, so I ask for the confirmation date on every one, and I treat a seller who cannot produce it as information in itself.

The second is the road. Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails that test generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition: the house can stand and be repaired, but its replacement cannot. Exceptions and approvals exist, so I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation rather than measuring a street on a map and guessing. A cheap house that passes both checks is a supply story. A cheap house that fails one is a different purchase wearing the same price tag.

What happens between offer and keys?

Before you sign, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — that is a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. On a Kitakyushu purchase, that document is where the confirmation date, the road status, and the fees are all supposed to be on the table, and I would not sign a contract that skipped it.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening. A buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash — standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan. I treat the timeline as a planning range, not a promise.

Costs: what the price tells you, and what it does not

The brokerage fee is the one big cost I could calculate from the price alone — and at Kitakyushu prices, the rule that usually bites is the low-price one: for properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, the brokerage ceiling is ¥330,000 including tax. Above ¥4,000,000 the general cap is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. Either way, the cap is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so I ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately.

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027. The judicial scrivener who handles the registration is a separate cost: the national scrivener federation's fee survey puts the average for an ownership-transfer registration at around ¥57,000, tax included, for a standard case — your quote varies by office and case.

Two costs keep running after the purchase. Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value, billed to whoever owns the property on January 1 and normally paid in four instalments across the year; city planning tax can add up to 0.3% in urbanisation-promotion areas. And the acquisition tax is the bill people forget: it does not arrive at the closing table — the prefecture sends a payment notice some months later, commonly about three to six months, varying by prefecture.

What about earthquake insurance?

Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends only on prefecture and structure class and is the same at every insurer — but the official premium table in my fact base names only the highest band and the lowest band, and Fukuoka is in neither of the named lists, so I will not print a Kitakyushu premium figure I cannot source. Fire insurance premiums are set freely by each insurer, and no official figure exists for those either. What I can do — and did — is understand the mechanism before asking an insurer for the actual quote.

Two deadlines after you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

If the plan is to rent it out

Kitakyushu is not a city I can manage a property in from overseas by myself, so I checked what management companies charge. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. I make no promise about what rent a given property will achieve — that depends on the property and the local market, and it is exactly the kind of figure I will not print without a source. In a high-vacancy city that refusal matters twice over: the supply that makes prices low is the same supply a tenant chooses from.

On the tax side, rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family. Withholding is not the final tax: the owner files a Japanese return, and before filing, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office.
