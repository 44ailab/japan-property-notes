Minpaku Rules in Japan: Short-Term Rental Rules for Property Owners

Before I assumed a Japanese property could earn short-term rental income, I would check whether minpaku is allowed at that specific property. The Private Lodging Business Act uses a prior notification system and sets an annual operating cap of 180 days. An owner living abroad must also delegate management to a registered management company. Local ordinances can impose further restrictions, and condominium bylaws can prohibit minpaku altogether.

Notification and the annual cap

Minpaku under the Private Lodging Business Act requires notification to the relevant government authority before operating. This is a notification system under that Act, rather than a hotel business licence.

The national annual operating cap under this system is 180 days. I would treat that as a ceiling, not as a promise that a particular property can operate for that many days. Local restrictions and building rules can narrow what is possible.

If a listing looks attractive because of an assumed short-term rental plan, I would ask the licensed agent and a relevant professional to check the rules for the address before relying on that plan. A property’s appeal as a place to stay does not establish that it can be operated as minpaku.

What an overseas owner needs to arrange

For an absentee owner, including an owner living abroad, management must be delegated to a registered housing accommodation management company. I would ask a professional to confirm what that arrangement would involve for the property I am considering.

That requirement belongs in the discussion before purchase. It affects how an overseas owner can operate under the system, rather than being a detail to settle only after finding guests.

I would also ask how the manager would check the applicable national and local requirements. The aim is to understand the actual operating arrangement for the property, including its restrictions, before building an investment plan around it.

Check local ordinances

National rules are only part of the picture. Local governments can impose additional restrictions through ordinances, including limits affecting residential areas or particular days.

I would not assume that the rules for one Japanese municipality apply to another. Nor would I assume that a property can operate up to the national cap just because the cap exists. The relevant local authority or a qualified professional can help establish which ordinance applies to the address and what it permits.

Local restrictions are not specified here because they depend on the municipality. I would ask for the applicable rule to be identified rather than rely on a general description of the area.

Check condominium bylaws

If the property is in a condominium, I would also ask to see the building’s management bylaws. Those rules can prohibit minpaku outright.

This is a separate check from the national notification system and local ordinances. Even if a prospective owner understands the government requirements, the building’s own rules still matter to the proposed use.

For me, that makes the bylaws a document to review before buying, not a detail to infer from a listing. A licensed agent or relevant professional can help obtain and examine the applicable documents.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • Is minpaku permitted at this address under the applicable local ordinance?
  • Does the condominium’s management agreement or bylaws prohibit it?
  • What notification would be required before operating?
  • Which registered management company could an overseas owner appoint?
  • What operating restrictions would apply to this specific property?

FAQ

Is minpaku under the Private Lodging Business Act a licensing system?

It is a prior notification system under that Act, rather than a hotel business licence.

Can I operate for the full 180 days wherever I buy?

Not necessarily. Local ordinances can restrict operation further, and condominium bylaws can prohibit minpaku.

Do I need a registered manager if I live abroad?

Yes. An absentee owner living abroad must delegate management to a registered housing accommodation management company.
