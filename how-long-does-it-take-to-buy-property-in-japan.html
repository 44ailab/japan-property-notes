How Long Does It Take to Buy Property in Japan? 1–3 Months From Offer to Handover

Last reviewed: November 25, 2026

From offer to handover, the honest range is about one to three months — practitioner guides converge on that, and cash purchases sit at the faster end because there is no loan screening.
Inside that range, the contract-to-settlement stretch alone is "a few weeks to a few months" in the Real Estate Transaction Promotion Organization's handbook.
The purchase is not the end of the calendar: a non-resident's FEFTA report is due within 20 days of acquiring the property, and the acquisition-tax bill arrives some months after handover.

Quick answer

Plan on about one to three months from offer to handover. The stages are: offer and negotiation, the important-matters explanation and contract (with the deposit), then settlement and registration, then handover. What stretches the timeline is usually financing — a cash purchase skips loan screening entirely — and the paperwork a non-resident buyer needs, not the contract steps themselves.

I wanted a single number when I started — "how long does it take?" — and the sources refused to give me one. What they gave me instead was a range with reasons attached, which turned out to be more useful: once I could see which stage eats the time, I could see which parts of the wait were mine to shorten and which were not.

Stage one: offer to contract

After an offer is accepted, the next formal step is the important-matters explanation. A licensed agent must deliver it before signing — a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy — and it is the document where the property's legal status, restrictions, and costs are set out. I read it as the last cheap exit: questions cost nothing here and get expensive later.

At the contract, you pay the deposit. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. If the seller is a licensed brokerage, it may not take a deposit of more than 20%.

Stage two: contract to settlement

The Real Estate Transaction Promotion Organization's handbook puts contract to settlement at "a few weeks to a few months". In that window the balance is prepared, the registration documents are assembled, and — if there is a loan — the lender's screening runs. That screening is the single biggest variable I found: standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan, so a buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash, and a cash purchase removes the slowest stage from the calendar entirely.

At settlement, the ownership transfer is registered, normally by a judicial scrivener. There is no official scrivener fee schedule — each office sets its own fees. The national survey average for an ownership-transfer registration example is ¥56,678 including tax, which I use as a sense-check on a quote rather than as the quote.

Paying from abroad adds its own small delays, and I plan for them rather than discovering them: the money is normally wired from abroad to the seller or scrivener side as the contract directs, and banks run anti-money-laundering checks, so having bank statements and a record of where the money came from ready tends to matter more than the transfer itself. There is no official fee table for international wires, so I ask about costs rather than assume them.

Stage three: the calendar after handover

Two dates follow you home. The first is short: since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — I would confirm who is filing before the 20 days start running, not after. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

The second date is easy to forget because it is months away: the real estate acquisition tax does not arrive at the closing table. The prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase — in practice commonly about three to six months later, with timing varying by prefecture. I put it in the budget the day I sign, because a bill that arrives half a year later still arrives.

And the annual cycle starts with ownership itself: fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value to whoever owns the property on January 1, and the bills are normally paid in four instalments across the year. Which January 1 you own the property on decides your first bill, so it is worth knowing before you choose a settlement date.

What I would not promise

I would not promise anyone a specific number of weeks. The one-to-three-month range comes from practitioner guidance and the RETIO handbook's contract-to-settlement range, and the individual case — the property's title, the seller's situation, your financing, your documents — moves you within it. What I can say is where the time goes, and that is usually the question underneath the question.
