Japan Fixed Asset Tax for Foreign Property Owners

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value.
City planning tax applies only in urbanisation-promotion areas, at a municipal rate capped at 0.3%.
The owner as of January 1 is the relevant owner for the annual fixed asset tax.

When I began estimating the cost of owning Japanese property, I wanted to turn the purchase price into an annual tax figure. The fact-check points to a different starting value: the government’s assessed value. It also distinguishes fixed asset tax from city planning tax.

That means I would not multiply the listing price by a combined rate and call the result a bill. I would ask for the relevant assessed and taxable values, the property’s location and any applicable relief.

Fixed asset tax

Fixed asset tax is charged at the standard rate of 1.4% of the government's assessed value.

It is a municipal tax. The assessed value is reviewed every three years, and the owner as of January 1 is the relevant owner for the year. I would ask the municipality or a qualified professional how the current assessment and any applicable adjustments affect the property I am considering.

The standard rate is a reference in the fact-check. The actual bill requires the applicable taxable base and the municipality’s treatment.

City planning tax

City planning tax is separate. It applies only in urbanisation-promotion areas, and the municipality sets the rate up to a 0.3% cap.

The cap is not a statement that every property is charged 0.3%. I would ask whether the property is in an area where the tax applies and what rate the municipality uses.

Residential-land relief

The fact-check notes reduced taxable bases for residential land. Because eligibility and calculation depend on the property, I would ask the municipality or a tax professional whether a reduction applies and how it is reflected in the estimate.

That question matters because an assessed value and a final taxable base are not necessarily interchangeable. I would request the figures and relief separately rather than relying on a single unexplained annual total.

Paying from overseas

Bills are normally paid in four instalments across the year, with months varying by municipality. An owner abroad should establish how notices will be received and how payments will be made.

I would ask:

  • Which municipality issues the bill?
  • What assessed and taxable values are being used?
  • Does city planning tax apply, and at what rate?
  • Is residential-land relief reflected in the estimate?
  • Where will notices be sent, and what payment arrangements are available?

The fixed asset and city planning tax overview reproduces the relevant municipal-tax framework. For a particular property, I would seek the current assessment and local information rather than extrapolating from a national example.

Keep ownership taxes distinct

Real estate acquisition tax is a separate one-time prefectural tax that can be billed after purchase. Fixed asset tax is an ongoing ownership item. If the property is rented, income tax and possible withholding are separate questions again.

My ownership budget would therefore keep these items on different lines and identify when each is expected. That makes it easier to see which figures are estimates, which are based on a notice and which still need professional confirmation.
