Registering a Domestic Contact Person for Your Japan Property

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

This is the registration rule I nearly missed, because it does not come with the same paperwork fanfare as the taxes. When you register a property in Japan and you have no address in Japan yourself, the registry needs someone in Japan it can reach — a domestic contact person. I only found the rule when I went back through the registration requirements after buying, checking what an overseas owner is supposed to keep current. If you own, or are about to own, Japanese property from abroad, this is the page I needed then.

Quick answer

Since April 1, 2024, a titleholder who has no address in Japan registers a domestic contact person — someone in Japan who can be reached about the property — as part of the registration. Separately, from April 1, 2026, keeping your registered address and name current becomes a duty in itself: an address or name change must be registered within two years, with a fine of up to ¥50,000 for unjustified failure. Neither rule is a tax, and neither blocks a purchase; both are part of what owning from overseas actually involves.

Why the registry wants a person in Japan

The registry's problem is simple: an owner who cannot be reached is an owner whose property can drift into limbo — tax notices unanswered, problems unreported, and eventually a title nobody can deal with. The domestic contact is the registry's answer for owners whose own address is overseas. It is not an agent, not a manager, and not someone who can act for you in a transaction; it is a point of contact inside Japan attached to your registration. When I registered, the practical question was simply who that person would be — in practice, the professionals already involved in an overseas purchase, such as the judicial scrivener who handled the registration, are the people buyers discuss this with, and I would raise it before the registration is filed rather than after.

The second duty: keeping the registration current

The change I had not appreciated is that the registration is not a one-time event. From April 1, 2026, an owner whose registered address or name changes must register that change within two years, and unjustified failure carries a fine of up to ¥50,000. For an owner living abroad — the person most likely to move house, and the least likely to hear about a Japanese registration rule — this is the duty most easily missed. I now keep a note of it alongside the property's other recurring obligations: if I move, the registration moves with me, on a two-year clock.

It sits in a family of similar duties I have had to learn as an overseas owner. Inheritance registration has been mandatory since April 1, 2024 — within three years of learning of the inheritance, with a fine of up to ¥100,000. And from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents; the nationality is held in an internal file and does not appear on the public registry certificate. None of these are reasons not to buy. All of them are reasons to treat the registration as a living record rather than a certificate in a drawer.

How this fits the rest of owning from overseas

The contact-person rule is the registry-side version of a pattern that runs through everything an overseas owner does. The tax system asks for the same thing in its own form: before filing a Japanese return, a non-resident must appoint a tax agent in Japan and file that appointment with the tax office — rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding by the payer, except when the tenant is an individual renting the property as a home for themselves or their family, and the final return is how that withholding is settled. And a non-resident buyer has one more filing of their own: since April 1, 2026, acquiring real property in Japan means reporting to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount.

Day to day, the property itself is usually in other hands anyway. An MLIT survey of rental management companies found that the most common fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, which makes 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure for standard full management. The domestic contact, the tax agent, and the management company are three different roles — I keep them separate in my own records, because confusing them is how a notice ends up with the wrong person.
