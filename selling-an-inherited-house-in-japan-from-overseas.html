How Do I Sell an Inherited House in Japan From Overseas?

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The letter arrived at my address overseas: a relative had died, and there was a house in Japan with my name somewhere in its future. My first assumption was that selling it started with finding a buyer. It does not. When I worked through the order of operations, the sale turned out to be the fourth step, not the first — registration comes before it, a tax agent comes before filing anything, and one valuable deduction runs on deadlines that do not wait for a family to finish grieving. This page is the sequence as I pieced it together, with the detailed rules linked rather than repeated.

Quick answer

You sell an inherited house in Japan from overseas in this order: register the inheritance in your name first (mandatory since April 1, 2024, within three years of learning of the inheritance), appoint a tax agent in Japan, check the vacant-house special deduction's conditions and deadlines before you market the property, then sell — knowing the buyer normally withholds 10.21% of the price — and settle the final tax in a Japanese return. Residency alone does not disqualify an heir from the deduction; the conditions and the calendar do the deciding.

Step 1: the registration comes first

Since April 1, 2024, inheritance registration is mandatory in Japan: the heir must register the property in their name within three years of learning of the inheritance, with a fine of up to ¥100,000 for failing without justification. A house still registered in the deceased's name is not a house you can cleanly sell, so this is step one, not paperwork for later. Which country's law governs the succession itself is a separate question — in principle the deceased's national law — and that part belongs with a specialist, not with this page. The full rule is on this hub's inheritance registration page, linked below.

Step 2: appoint a tax agent before anything is filed

A non-resident who has to file or pay tax in Japan appoints a tax agent (a Japan-resident representative) and notifies the competent tax office. The inheritance tax return, if one is due, runs on its own clock — return and payment within ten months from the day after learning of the death — and Japan-situs property is taxable regardless of the residency or nationality of the deceased or the heirs, with a basic deduction of ¥30,000,000 plus ¥6,000,000 per statutory heir. I am not going to walk through inheritance tax computation here; the hub's inheritance tax page carries it, and an actual estate belongs with a professional. The point of this step is simpler: from overseas, every filing that follows moves through the tax agent, so the appointment comes early.

Step 3: check the vacant-house deduction before you market the house

This is the step with a clock on it. The vacant-house special deduction lets an heir who sells an inherited house deduct ¥30,000,000 from the gain — ¥20,000,000 per heir when three or more heirs inherit — but only inside a set of conditions: the house was built on or before May 31, 1981 (condominiums are excluded), the deceased lived there alone (with a care-facility exception), the sale price is ¥100,000,000 or less, and the sale completes by December 31 of the year three years after the inheritance began. The program itself ends on December 31, 2027. A municipal confirmation certificate is required, and since the 2024 change, demolition or seismic retrofit can be done by the buyer by February 15 of the year after the sale. Residency alone does not disqualify: the statute speaks of an individual who inherited, with no residency exclusion — filing runs through the tax agent, with the municipal certificate in hand. Whether a specific house qualifies is a conditions check, not a promise, and it has to happen before the sale is structured, because several conditions are about timing and state of the house at sale. The hub's vacant-house page carries the full conditions.

Step 4: the sale, and the 10.21% that leaves the price first

When a non-resident sells Japanese land or buildings, the buyer must withhold 10.21% of the sale price and pay it to the tax office by the 10th of the following month. The one exemption is narrow: an individual buyer purchasing the property as a home for themselves or their family, at a price of ¥100,000,000 or less. An investor buyer does not fit it, so on a typical sale the withholding happens. It is a prepayment, not the final tax — which is why step 5 exists.

Step 5: the final return settles it

The final tax is capital gains on the actual gain, at the non-resident rates: 30.63% if the property was held five years or less (measured as of January 1 of the sale year), 15.315% if held longer. Non-residents do not pay inhabitant tax on top. The seller files a Japanese return — through the tax agent — the withheld 10.21% is credited, and any excess is refunded. I state the deduction and these rates as the rules I verified; how they land on one family's numbers is a computation for their tax adviser, and I would not take a figure for a specific estate from a web page, including this one.

Sources: Ministry of Justice: inheritance registration duty, National Tax Agency Tax Answers No. 3306 (vacant-house special deduction), No. 2884 (payments to non-residents) and the non-resident capital-gains guidance, as set out in this hub's fact base.
