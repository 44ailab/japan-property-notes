Buying Property in Kyoto as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The buying rules in Kyoto are the national ones — no visa or residency is required, and ownership gives no visa.
What changes in Kyoto is the housing stock: many listings are older wooden houses, so two checks mattered more to me here than the price — the building confirmation date against the 1981 seismic standard, and whether the plot's road frontage would even allow a rebuild.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy property in Kyoto under the same national rules as the rest of Japan. For older houses — a large share of what is listed in Kyoto — the two checks I would not skip are the building confirmation date (the seismic standard changed in 1981) and the road frontage (a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres, or rebuilding is generally not allowed). For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax.

I came to Kyoto's listings for the same reason most people do — the old wooden townhouses. What I did not expect was how much of my checking time would go on questions that have nothing to do with the price: when exactly was this building confirmed, and what road does the plot actually touch? This page is those two checks, plus the national basics that sit underneath every purchase.

Check one: the building confirmation date

Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, and the date that matters is the building confirmation date — the date the design was confirmed under the standard then in force — not simply the year the house was finished. Two houses that look the same age can sit on different sides of that line.

I ask for the confirmation date on any older Kyoto house before I think about the price. A pre-change building is not automatically a bad purchase, but it is a different risk and a different renovation conversation than a post-change one, and I want to know which I am looking at. The agent's important-matters explanation is where this should be confirmed in writing.

Check two: the road the plot touches

Under Article 43 of the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition — the existing house can stand and be repaired, but if it comes down, a new one generally cannot go up. Kyoto's older districts have plenty of narrow lanes, so this is not an edge case here; it is one of the first things I check on a listing.

Exceptions exist — approvals under Article 43(2), and setback arrangements on certain narrow legal roads — so I do not reject a property on the listing text alone. I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation, which a licensed agent must deliver before signing as a legal duty, not a courtesy.

The national basics underneath

None of the buying mechanics are Kyoto-specific. No permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own, and ownership grants no right to stay in Japan. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign.

Registration and license tax is charged on the fixed-asset-tax assessed value, not the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, and its payment notice arrives from the prefecture some months after the purchase rather than at the closing table. On an older house, the assessed values can be far below the listing price, which is exactly why I ask for them instead of estimating taxes from the price.

Two more things I check on any older-house listing: whether the land is freehold or leasehold, because both exist in Japan and they are not the same purchase; and whether the seller's story about the title is simple, because if a foreign owner dies, the property still has to be registered in the heirs' names — inheritance registration has been mandatory since April 1, 2024, within three years of learning of the inheritance.

After you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

From October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must also declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality does not appear on the public registry certificate.
