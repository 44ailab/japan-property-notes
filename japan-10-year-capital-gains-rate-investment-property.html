Does Japan's 10-Year Capital Gains Tax Rate Apply to Investment Property?

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

I first met the 10.21% figure in a piece of marketing copy about holding Japanese property long-term, and for one pleasant minute I believed it: hold for ten years, sell, and pay barely ten percent on the gain. Then I read the conditions, and the minute ended. The 10.21% rate is real — and it belongs to a different seller than me. It is a reduced rate for a home the seller personally lived in for over ten years. An investment property does not become eligible by being held, however patiently. This page exists because the confusion is common, and expensive if it survives into a budget.

Quick answer

No. The 10.21% rate applies to the first ¥60 million of taxable gain on a property used as the seller's own residence for more than ten years, sold within three years of moving out. It does not apply to investment property. A non-resident investor's actual long-term rate is 15.315% on the gain for a property held over five years (30.63% at five years or less) — a genuinely lower rate than the short-term one, just not the residential one.

The condition I had missed: whose home it was

The reduced rate's conditions are about the seller's life, not the property's holding period alone. The property must have been used as the seller's own residence for over ten years, and the sale must happen within three years of the seller moving out. A property bought to rent out fails the first condition on day one and keeps failing it for as long as it is rented — ten years of tenancy is ten years of the property being someone else's home, not the owner's. Holding period is necessary for the relief but nowhere near sufficient; the residence use is the gate, and an investment property does not pass through it.

What an investor actually gets for holding longer

The honest long-term benefit for an investor is the step between the two non-resident capital-gains rates: 30.63% on a property held five years or less, measured as of January 1 of the sale year, and 15.315% once the holding passes five years. Non-residents do not pay inhabitant tax on top of either. That is the number to put in an exit budget — along with the mechanics that surround it: the buyer withholds 10.21% of the gross sale price at the transaction (exempt only when the buyer is an individual purchasing a home for themselves or family at ¥100,000,000 or less), and the final return credits the withholding against the actual gains tax. I note the coincidence because it causes real confusion: the withholding rate and the residential reduced rate are both 10.21%, and they have nothing to do with each other. One is a prepayment collected from every non-resident seller; the other is a relief most investors will never qualify for.

Why this page is on an investment hub at all

Because the misconception travels in the investor's direction. The residential rate appears in general guides to selling property in Japan without its conditions attached, and an investor who lifts it into a spreadsheet understates their exit tax by a third. My rule on this hub is that a rate is only printed with the conditions that gate it — and where a popular figure does not apply to the reader this hub serves, saying so plainly is worth a page. If you are selling a home you actually lived in, the relief may be yours and the hub's capital-gains page links onward; if you are selling a rental, budget at 15.315% or 30.63% and let any surprise be a pleasant one.

Sources: National Tax Agency guidance on the reduced rate for long-held residential property and on non-resident capital gains (Tax Answer No. 2884 series) — as set out in this hub's fact base.
