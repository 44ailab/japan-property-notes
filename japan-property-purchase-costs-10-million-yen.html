Quick answer

For a ¥10 million property, the brokerage fee cap works out to ¥360,000 before consumption tax, and the reduced stamp duty for a sale contract at exactly ¥10 million is ¥5,000. Registration and acquisition taxes use assessed values, so the purchase price alone cannot produce a reliable total.

I wanted to know which costs could actually be calculated from a ¥10 million price and which ones needed another document. That distinction turned out to be more useful than adding a generic percentage to the listing price and calling the result a quote.

What the price can tell me

For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax.

For ¥10 million, that calculation gives ¥360,000 before consumption tax. It is a legal ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction. I would ask the brokerage for its actual fee and the consumption tax separately so I can see the complete amount.

The contract amount can also identify the relevant stamp-duty band. Under the National Tax Agency’s reduced table, a contract amount over ¥5 million through ¥10 million has ¥5,000 in stamp duty. The reduced rates apply to contracts created by March 31, 2027. The boundary matters: exactly ¥10 million is in this band, while an amount over ¥10 million moves to the next listed band.

What the price cannot tell me

Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value rather than the purchase price. For land transferred by sale, the rate is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029. For a building transferred by sale, the rate is 2.0%.

I would therefore ask for the assessed value of the land and the assessed value of the building. Without them, multiplying the purchase price by those rates would give an answer using the wrong base.

Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value. For acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, the reduced rate is 3% for land and houses; non-residential buildings remain at 4%. Building land can have its taxable base halved under the applicable rules. Other reductions exist, and I would ask a tax professional whether they apply instead of building them into my budget automatically.

The acquisition tax notice comes from the prefecture after purchase, not at the closing table. Practitioner guidance commonly describes a wait of around three to six months, with timing varying by prefecture. I would keep that later bill on the list even if the initial settlement statement looks complete.

Use the scrivener survey carefully

The national judicial-scrivener survey gives an average of ¥56,678 including tax for an ownership-transfer example involving one land parcel and one building with a combined assessed value of ¥10 million. That assessed value is not necessarily the same thing as this page’s ¥10 million purchase price.

There is no official scrivener fee schedule. Each office sets its own fees, so I would request a quote for the actual transaction and ask about document costs.

For a broader initial budget, the property type matters. On a used condo, the closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price (SUUMO's closing-cost guide). That is a planning reference for a used condo, not a guarantee or a substitute for the individual figures above.

Same ¥10 million price, two different bills: used house vs used condo

Two properties can both be priced at ¥10 million and still produce different closing bills, because only part of the bill is calculated from the price. I walked the same ¥10 million through a used wooden house and a used condo to see which lines stay identical and which lines move.

The lines that do not change. The brokerage fee cap is the same for both: 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax, which at ¥10 million is ¥360,000 before tax — ¥396,000 with consumption tax included. The stamp duty is also the same: a sale contract at exactly ¥10 million falls in the reduced-rate band of ¥5,000, for contracts created by March 31, 2027.

The lines that change shape. Registration and license tax uses the fixed-asset-tax assessed value for both property types — 1.5% on land transferred by sale, for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and 2.0% on the building. What differs is the base itself: a house is assessed as a whole land parcel plus a building, while a condo is assessed as one unit plus a share of the land under the building. Same rates, different assessed values — so this line cannot be totalled from the ¥10 million price for either property. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, and the taxable base for building land is halved. It is also billed later: the prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase, commonly about three to six months later, and the timing varies by prefecture.

The scrivener benchmark fits the house pattern. The national survey average of ¥56,678 including tax is for an ownership transfer on one land parcel and one building — the house pattern above. I would not assume the same figure for a condo; I would ask for a quote for the actual transaction.

The clearest annual difference is earthquake insurance. Earthquake insurance is an add-on to fire insurance. You set the cover at 30–50% of the fire insurance amount, capped at ¥50,000,000 for the building. That percentage is the cover limit you choose — not a guaranteed payout. The premium depends on the prefecture and the structure class, and per ¥10,000,000 of cover per year, before discounts, the Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa and Shizuoka band charges ¥41,100 for a wooden building and ¥27,500 for a non-wooden one. In the lowest band the figures are ¥11,200 and ¥7,300. A wooden house and a non-wooden condo sit in different structure classes, so this line differs every year, not just at purchase.

Planning totals. On a used condo, the closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price (SUUMO's closing-cost guide) — at ¥10 million, a planning range of ¥600,000 to ¥800,000. On a used home, buyers commonly budget about 5–10% of the price for the closing costs, including the brokerage fee (SUUMO's closing-cost guide) — at ¥10 million, ¥500,000 to ¥1,000,000. These are planning references, not quotes; the assessed-value taxes are what move a real transaction inside those ranges.

Three questions ¥10 million buyers ask

How much cash do I need on top of the ¥10 million price?

Three lines can be calculated from the price itself: the brokerage fee cap of ¥396,000 including consumption tax, stamp duty of ¥5,000 on a contract at exactly ¥10 million, and the national-survey scrivener average of ¥56,678 including tax. Together those three come to ¥457,678. The registration and acquisition taxes sit on top of that, and because both use assessed values rather than the price, no honest total can be produced until you have the assessed values. As a planning range, SUUMO puts closing costs on a used condo at about 6–8% of the price, and buyers of used homes commonly budget about 5–10% of the price including the brokerage fee.

When do the taxes arrive?

The stamp duty attaches to the sale contract itself. The registration and license tax is calculated on the assessed values when the ownership transfer is registered. The acquisition tax is the late one: it does not arrive at the closing table. The prefecture sends a payment notice after the purchase — in practice some months later, commonly about three to six months, with timing varying by prefecture — so I would keep money aside for it even after the settlement statement looks complete.

How much deposit do I pay, and what happens to it?

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price, which at ¥10 million is ¥500,000 to ¥1,000,000. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign.
