Important Matters Explanation in Japan: What Art. 35 Must Put on the Table Before You Sign

Last reviewed: November 22, 2026

Before a Japanese property sale contract is signed, there is a formal step that has no real equivalent in many countries: the agent sits down with a document and explains the property's legal position, item by item, before anyone signs anything. I went through what the law requires that explanation to cover, because for a buyer overseas it is the single most protective step in the whole purchase — it is where the road status, the building confirmation date, and the fees are all put on the table while you can still walk away. One boundary first: this explanation is delivered by the licensed brokerage, never by me. Japan Property Notes makes introductions only.

Quick answer

Under Article 35 of the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation before the sale contract is signed. It is a legal duty, not a courtesy, and a contract signing that skips it is not a shortcut — it is a missing legal step. Among the items it must put on the table are the road status of the plot, the building confirmation date, and the fees, including the brokerage fee within its legal cap.

The road: the check that decides whether a house can be rebuilt

Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails that test generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition: the house can stand and be repaired, but its replacement cannot. For a buyer looking at photographs from another country, this is invisible — a lane in a photo tells you nothing about its legal width. The explanation is where the agent confirms the road status on the record, and it is the first item I listen for.

The confirmation date: which seismic standard the house was built under

Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, and was tightened again in 2000. Which side of those changes a house falls on is decided by its building confirmation date — not by the year in a listing headline, which may describe a renovation or a relisting. The confirmation date is stated in the explanation, and on an older house I ask for it every time; a seller or agent who cannot produce it is information in itself.

The fees: agreed in the open, inside a legal cap

The explanation is also where the money is set out before signing: the deposit (set by agreement, commonly 5–10% of the price) and the brokerage fee. The fee has a legal cap — 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax for a price above ¥4,000,000, and a ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax for a price of ¥8,000,000 or less. The cap is a ceiling, not necessarily the fee quoted in a particular transaction, so the figure to listen for is the brokerage's actual fee and its consumption tax, stated separately, before you sign rather than after.
