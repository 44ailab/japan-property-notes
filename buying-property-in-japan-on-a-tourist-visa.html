Buying Property in Japan on a Tourist Visa

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The plan sounds tidy: fly in as a tourist, view properties, sign, fly home an owner. I have heard it from buyers who assumed the purchase would need a special visa — and from a few who assumed the opposite, that owning the property would somehow let them stay on in it. Both assumptions are wrong, in opposite directions, and I made the first one myself when I started checking. Buying property in Japan on a tourist visa is possible because no visa is involved in buying at all. This page separates what a buying trip can do from what ownership will never do.

Quick answer

You do not need any particular visa to buy property in Japan — no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own, whether you sign while visiting or from overseas. Being in Japan as a tourist is simply one way to be present for viewings and signing. The limit runs the other way: owning property gives you no visa and no right to stay in Japan, and it does not extend a tourist stay. Buy the property because the property works; plan your stays under the normal entry rules.

Why the tourist-visa question dissolves once you check it

Japan has no status of residence for property owners, and no permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own. That is the whole answer to the visa question: the purchase system does not ask what stamp is in your passport, because ownership is registered, not permitted. A tourist trip is therefore a perfectly workable setting for the parts of a purchase that benefit from being present — viewing, meeting the brokerage, and the important-matters explanation, the document a licensed agent must deliver before you sign.

What "non-resident" means in the rules that follow is decided by where you live, not by your passport or your entry stamp. A Japanese citizen living abroad can be a non-resident, and a foreign national living and working in Japan is generally a resident. A short buying trip does not change which side of that line you are on — I checked my own position before travelling, because it decides whether the 20-day report below applies.

What ownership will not do for your stay

I want to be blunt about this half, because it is the half that costs people money when they get it wrong: owning the property gives you no right to stay in it beyond what the entry rules allow anyone. There is no owner visa, no residence-by-purchase route, and no extension that comes with the keys. If living in Japan is part of the plan, that is a separate immigration question with its own routes, and the purchase answers none of it. I treat the property as an investment that I visit under the normal rules, and I would advise anyone to write their plans the same way.

Practical shape of a buying trip

From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end because there is no loan screening — and a buyer who lives abroad should plan on cash, since standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan. One trip rarely covers the whole span, and it does not need to: the deposit is set by agreement, commonly 5–10% of the price, and the later steps can be handled from overseas. You do not need a Japanese bank account at any point — a person who lives outside Japan generally cannot open an ordinary one, and payment is normally wired from abroad to the seller or scrivener side as the contract directs.

Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying the deposit loses it, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign — especially the parts about dates, since a purchase that straddles a flight home needs its deadlines written down, not remembered.

The filing that follows you home

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition. Acquisitions on or after that date are reportable regardless of purpose or amount. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it — but I would confirm who is filing it rather than assume, particularly if I had already flown home. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

Separately, from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike, and the nationality is held in an internal file — it does not appear on the public registry certificate.

Sources: Ministry of Finance: FEFTA reporting for non-residents acquiring real property, Ministry of Justice: nationality declaration at registration.
