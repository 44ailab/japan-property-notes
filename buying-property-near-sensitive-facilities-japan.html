Buying Property Near a Military Base or Sensitive Facility in Japan

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The headline version of this rule is frightening: Japan restricts land sales near military bases, foreigners beware. I read versions of that claim in three places before I went and checked the law itself, because a plot near a base or a power facility was exactly the kind of thing I might otherwise have walked away from — or worse, bought without knowing. What I found is narrower and calmer than the headlines, and it applies to Japanese buyers exactly as much as to me. This page is the rule as it stands, stated carefully.

Quick answer

There is no ban on foreigners buying property near a military base or other sensitive facility in Japan. Under the Act on the Survey and Regulation of Land Use Around Important Facilities, if the land or building sits inside a designated Special Watch Zone and the transfer is of 200㎡ or more, both the seller and the buyer must notify the Prime Minister in advance. It is a notification, not an approval — the government is told about the transfer; it does not approve or refuse it — and it applies regardless of nationality.

What the rule actually is

The law designates areas around important facilities — the category covers facilities such as defence-related sites and other critical infrastructure — as watch zones. Inside the narrower Special Watch Zone, a transfer of land or a building of 200㎡ or more triggers the advance notification. Both sides of the transaction file it: the seller notifies, and the buyer notifies. Below 200㎡, this particular rule does not trigger at all.

I checked the two misunderstandings that travel with this rule. The first is that it is aimed at foreigners: it is not — the duty follows the land and the transaction, and a Japanese buyer of the same plot files the same notification. The second is that notification means the state can stop the sale: under the current law it is a reporting step, and I found no approval or refusal attached to it. Treating it as a ban would have meant ruling out whole districts on the strength of a headline.

What this means in practice for a buyer

Three practical consequences followed for me. First, location research now includes a zone question: before I take a plot seriously, I ask the brokerage whether it falls inside a designated Special Watch Zone. Zone designations are published, and the agent's important-matters explanation — the document a licensed agent must deliver before you sign — is where land-use restrictions of this kind belong. Second, if the answer is yes and the plot is 200㎡ or more, I build the notification into the timeline rather than discovering it at the contract table; it is one more filing, handled before the transfer, not a reason to abandon the purchase. Third, I keep the threshold in mind when comparing plots: two otherwise similar properties can sit on opposite sides of the 200㎡ line.

Everything else about the purchase stays the national process. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end.

The rules that apply to every purchase still apply here

No permission, visa, or residency is required to buy or own property in Japan, and ownership itself grants no right to stay in Japan. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax; for properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies.

Two filings follow a non-resident buyer home regardless of where the property sits. Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount; not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000. And from October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration — a separate step from the watch-zone notification, and one that applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike.
