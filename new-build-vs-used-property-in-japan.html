New-Build vs Used Property in Japan

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

The process is one process: the same important-matters explanation, the same deposit mechanics, and the same two deadlines afterwards, whichever side you buy.
What changes is where the money and the risk sit — a new condo usually carries no brokerage fee, while a used property's risks live in documents a new build does not have yet: the road, the 1981 line, and, on a condo, the last owner's unpaid fees.
This is the comparison I built from the verified figures, including which of the headline cost bands actually survive contact with the fee schedule.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy either new or used property in Japan on the same national rules — no visa, residency, or Japanese bank account required, and ownership grants no visa. The cost bands differ: on a used condo, the closing costs commonly add up to about 6–8% of the price (SUUMO's closing-cost guide); on a used home, buyers commonly budget about 5–10% of the price for the closing costs, including the brokerage fee (SUUMO's closing-cost guide); a new condo runs lower, about 3–5%, because there is usually no brokerage fee. Against that, a used property carries three document checks a new build does not: the building confirmation date against the 1981 seismic change, the road-access rule on houses, and a previous owner's unpaid condo fees. A deduction of ¥12,000,000 exists for qualifying newly built homes under the acquisition tax — I name it because it exists, not as a promise that a given purchase qualifies.

I started this comparison expecting the answer to be a price per square metre. It turned into a comparison of two different piles of paper: the new build's pile is mostly invoices, and the used property's pile is mostly history. What follows is the money first, then the history, then the parts that refuse to differ.

The cost bands, honestly labelled

The bands above come from SUUMO's closing-cost guides, and the single biggest reason for the gap between them is one line: the brokerage fee. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax, where a brokerage is involved at all. A new condo is typically sold by the developer directly, so that line is usually absent — which is most of why the new-condo band sits at about 3–5% while a used condo, bought through a brokerage, sits at about 6–8%. A used house adds the widest spread, about 5–10% including the brokerage fee, because the properties and their paperwork vary the most. The bands are planning figures from a dated guide, not quotes; the fee schedule underneath them is law, and the two should not be confused.

One tax line genuinely favours new. Real estate acquisition tax is charged on the assessed value at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, and a deduction of ¥12,000,000 exists for qualifying newly built homes. Deductions like this have conditions attached, and eligibility is the prefecture's call, not mine — I note that the deduction exists and ask about it early, and I do not net it into a budget until it is confirmed. Registration and license tax, by contrast, does not take sides: it is charged on the fixed-asset-tax assessed values either way, land transferred by sale at a reduced 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029 and a building transferred by sale at 2.0%.

The used side's three document risks

The first is seismic. Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, so on any used building I check the building confirmation date and treat a pre-change date as the start of the questions, not the end of them. A new build is confirmed under the current standard by construction; a used building asks you to do this reading yourself.

The second is the road, and it applies to houses. Under the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres, and a plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition. Exceptions exist, so I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation. A new-build house on a fresh plot rarely raises this; an older house on an old lane is where it lives.

The third is quieter, and it is condo-specific: a buyer can end up responsible for a previous owner's unpaid management and repair-reserve fees. I covered that in its own guide, and it is one of the first things I now verify on a used condo, because the listing price never mentions it. A new condo has no previous owner — but it has the mirror-image version of the same question, which is whether the association's repair-reserve plan and its scheduled increases are realistic, and I read those documents with the same suspicion in the other direction.

What does not change

The shared list is long. Before signing either contract, a licensed agent must deliver the important-matters explanation — a legal duty under the Building Lots and Buildings Transaction Business Act, not a courtesy. The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign. From offer to handover, practitioner guides put the timeline at roughly one to three months, with cash purchases at the faster end — and a non-resident buyer plans on cash either way, because standard Japanese home loans are for people who live and earn in Japan and there is no official program for non-residents.

Afterwards, the same two filings wait on both sides of this comparison. Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount; not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000. And from October 5, 2026, each individual who becomes a registered owner declares their nationality etc. at registration — new-build buyers included, Japanese and foreign alike. The acquisition tax bill also arrives the same way on both sides: not at the closing table, but from the prefecture some months later, commonly about three to six months.

If the plan is to rent the property out, that machinery is identical too: an MLIT survey found the most common management fee band is 3% to under 5% of the rent, making 3–5% of monthly rent the practical planning figure, and rent paid to a non-resident owner is subject to 20.42% withholding except when the tenant is an individual renting it as their own home. I make no promise about what rent either a new or a used property will achieve in a given city. My own lean, for what it is worth, is that the new-build premium buys the absence of the three document risks — nothing more than that — and whether the absence is worth the premium depends on how much of the used side's discount survives the three checks. Sometimes all of it does. That is the whole comparison, and it is only ever settled property by property.

Sources: SUUMO closing-cost guides (Recruit), National Tax Agency: registration and license tax, Ministry of Finance: FEFTA reporting for non-residents acquiring real property, Ministry of Justice: nationality declaration at registration, Building Standards Act Arts. 42–43 (road access).
