Buying an Akiya in Japan as a Foreigner

Last reviewed: November 21, 2026

Japan counted 9,002,000 vacant homes in its 2023 housing survey — 13.8% of all homes — so the akiya (empty house) market is real, and foreigners can buy into it on the same rules as any other property.
What I could not find, anywhere official, is a price series for akiya. The survey counts homes; it does not price them. Any page that gives you an average akiya price by region is using a number no official source publishes.
The checks that decided things for me were not about price at all: whether the land can legally be rebuilt on, and which side of the 1981 seismic change the building was confirmed on.

Quick answer

A foreigner can buy an akiya in Japan with no visa or residency requirement, under the same national rules as any property purchase. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate brokerage fee ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies. Before falling for a cheap listing, check the road frontage — a plot that does not touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres generally cannot be rebuilt — and check the building confirmation date against the 1981 seismic standard change.

I went into akiya listings expecting the work to be about finding a low price. It was not. The low prices were the easy part — they are on every listing. The work was finding out what a particular cheap house actually is in legal and physical terms, because that is what decides whether it is a bargain or a liability with a roof.

First, an honest word about prices

The Statistics Bureau's 2023 Housing and Land Survey counted 9,002,000 vacant homes as of October 1, 2023 — 13.8% of all homes in Japan — of which 3,856,000 were vacant with no rental, sale, or second-home use. That is the official picture, and it stops at counting. There is no official price series for akiya, by region or nationally.

So on this page I describe prices the only honest way available: they vary enormously, by location, by condition, and by whether the land can be rebuilt on, and a specific listing price is an example, never a statistic. A house listed for almost nothing is usually priced that way for a reason I need to identify — condition, location, or a legal limit on the land — not because the seller failed to notice its value.

The rebuild check comes before the price

Under Article 43 of the Building Standards Act, a building plot must touch a road at least 4 metres wide for at least 2 metres. A plot that fails this generally cannot be rebuilt after demolition. The house that stands there can be lived in and repaired, but if it comes down, a new one generally cannot replace it.

Older vacant houses sit on older, narrower plots more often than modern homes do, so this check is not a formality on an akiya — it is the first question I ask. Exceptions exist, including approvals under Article 43(2) and setback arrangements on certain narrow legal roads, so I have the agent confirm the road status in the important-matters explanation, which a licensed agent must deliver before signing as a legal duty.

The 1981 line

Japan's seismic standard changed in 1981, and many akiya were built and confirmed before that change. The date that matters is the building confirmation date, not just the year on the listing. A pre-change house is not automatically a write-off — but it is a different structural conversation, and the cost of bringing an old house up to a standard I am comfortable with is part of the real price, even though no survey can give me that figure in advance. I budget for an inspection and quotes on the actual house rather than trusting any general renovation number, because no official one exists.

The costs I could pin down

The brokerage fee on a cheap property surprised me, in a good way. For a property priced above ¥4,000,000, the legal cap on the brokerage fee is 3% of the price + ¥60,000 + consumption tax. For properties priced at ¥8,000,000 or less, a separate ceiling of ¥330,000 including tax applies. On a very cheap house, that ceiling is the number to check the quote against.

Registration and license tax is charged on the fixed-asset-tax assessed value, not the purchase price — land transferred by sale is reduced to 1.5% for registrations made by March 31, 2029, and a building transferred by sale is 2.0%. Real estate acquisition tax also uses assessed value, at a reduced 3% for land and houses for acquisitions made by March 31, 2027, and its bill arrives from the prefecture some months after the purchase. On an old house the assessed values are often low, but I ask for them rather than guess, because they — not the listing price — set these two taxes. There is no official scrivener fee schedule either; the national survey average for an ownership-transfer registration is around ¥57,000 including tax, and each office sets its own fees, so I ask for a quote.

The deposit is set by agreement — commonly 5–10% of the price. Under the standard contract terms, a buyer who cancels after paying it loses the deposit, and a seller who cancels must repay double. The exact terms are in your contract, so check them before you sign.

After you buy

Since April 1, 2026, a non-resident who acquires real property in Japan must report to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of the acquisition, regardless of purpose or amount — an akiya purchase counts like any other. The report is in Japanese, and a Japan-resident agent such as the brokerage may submit it. Not reporting, or filing a false report, carries up to six months' imprisonment or a fine of up to ¥500,000.

From October 5, 2026, an individual who becomes a registered owner must also declare their nationality etc. as search information at registration, with supporting proof such as a passport copy for non-residents. It applies to Japanese and foreign buyers alike and does not appear on the public registry certificate.
